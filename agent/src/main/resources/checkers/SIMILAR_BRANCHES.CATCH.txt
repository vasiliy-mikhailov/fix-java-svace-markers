catch\s*\(\s*(?:final\s+)?[\w.]+(?:\s*\|\s*[\w.]+)*\s+\w+\s*\)
Two or more catch clauses of the SAME try statement have handler bodies that are identical after
whitespace normalization; the marker is anchored on one clause of the pair, usually the first and
most specific. The claim is only "these two handlers do the same thing": either the specific clause
is dead weight subsumed by the broader one, or one body is a copy-paste that is false for its own
exception. Do not confuse it with SIMILAR_BRANCHES (identical if/else bodies) or
SIMILAR_BRANCHES.SWITCH (identical case bodies); nor with the empty-catch and over-broad-catch
family (REC_CATCH_EXCEPTION, DE_MIGHT_IGNORE, "exception swallowed"), which judges what one handler
does rather than the duplication between two; nor is it "unreachable catch", since
specific-before-general compiles fine and is exactly the shape that gets flagged.

FIRST FIND THE PAIR. The marker names one clause; the finding lives in the whole try. Compare the
normalized body of every catch on that try. The twins need not be adjacent — a clause with a
different body can sit between them — and they are often a subtype together with its supertype, so
"similar" does not mean "same exception type".

THEN DECIDE WHICH OF TWO SETTLEMENTS YOU ARE IN, because only one is testable. Under honest
redundancy both handlers really do mean the same thing, every available fix (delete the subsumed
clause; fold the siblings into `catch (A | B e)`) is behaviour-preserving by construction, and no
test can separate before from after: say that plainly, do not manufacture a RED. Otherwise the
shared body is false for one branch — it asserts something true of only one exception — and that IS
observable through whatever the body emits: a return value, a message key, a log line, a status.

The second needs no new machinery, only two inputs that enter the same try and leave by different
clauses. Assert that the emitted outcomes DIFFER, or that the one taking the broad clause is not the
specific clause's outcome. Both coming back identical is a RED for the right reason, since the
condition the shared text names was never established on the second input. It goes GREEN when the
broad handler is given its own outcome — the fix direction the anchor line does NOT suggest, and the
one that leaves the specific clause and everything depending on it untouched. Craft the probe so it
lands on the clause you mean: an earlier statement in the same try, such as a decode or a resource
constructed in a try-with-resources head, can throw a third type and take a third clause.

WHAT GOES WRONG HERE.
- Asserting the marker back at itself: a test exercising the specific clause and asserting the
  shared outcome is green before AND after every candidate fix, because the twin returns the same
  thing. Same for reading the .java text or counting catch clauses reflectively.
- Claiming RED/GREEN for the merge. Deleting a subsumed clause leaves behaviour bit-identical; any
  test passing after also passed before. The correct write-up is "not observable", not a fake RED.
- Escalating into a rethrow, or dropping the broad handler, where that handler is what turns
  malformed input into a graceful failure rather than an unhandled 500 at the boundary; or rewriting
  both bodies, when one outcome is depended on by documentation or an existing test and has to
  survive the fix while only the other end moves.
- Reading intent off the surroundings. What the enclosing code is named, and what its package,
  directory or reputation suggests, does not make twin handlers a decision; only something checked
  in that DEPENDS on both clauses emitting the same outcome does — a doc saying so, a test asserting
  it for each exception, a comment or suppression naming the choice. Duplicated handler bodies are
  ordinary plumbing; whatever a file is for, the handlers are rarely the thing it is for.
- Verdict slip: "the bodies are identical" is confirmed by reading, and "the specific clause is
  therefore dead" follows — but neither "so nothing is wrong" nor "so a defect is observable" does.
  What decides it is whether the shared body is a true statement about BOTH exceptions.
